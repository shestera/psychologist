# Sexology Formulation Methods

Read this reference for a desire or arousal concern, shame about sexual
response, or a life-stage change affecting sexuality. These are
psychoeducational formulation models, not diagnostic instruments, treatment
protocols, or a universal sequence. Use only the smallest model that helps the
user understand a concrete concern or choose a next step.

## Circular and responsive desire

Do not assume that desire must appear spontaneously before any affectionate or
sexual context. For some people and situations, willingness to engage in a
freely chosen, potentially rewarding context can precede subjective arousal or
desire; for others, desire is spontaneous, mixed, absent, or follows another
pattern. Map the user's actual sequence, motivation, context, satisfaction, and
stop points rather than imposing a linear cycle.

Responsive desire never means that someone should begin or continue unwanted
activity, that consent can be inferred later, or that a partner may pressure
them to "see if desire appears." Basson's model was developed primarily from
women's experiences and must not be universalized by gender or treated as a
normative account.

## Dual control model

Use the dual control model as a collaborative map of excitation and inhibition:
what, in this context, makes sexual response more available, and what applies a
protective or distracting brake? Consider physical state, stress, safety,
attention, relationship context, novelty or familiarity, anticipated
consequences, and learned meanings. The balance can vary by person and
situation; a strong brake or weak accelerator is not a diagnosis or defect.

Do not reproduce, score, or interpret the SES/SIS questionnaires from memory.
If a formal measure could change care, verify its exact version, population,
language, rights, scoring, and evidence under the psychometric and evidence
policies. An informal accelerator-and-brake map is psychoeducation, not a test.

## Subjective and genital arousal non-concordance

Explain when useful that subjective arousal and genital response are related
but can diverge. A genital response does not establish desire, pleasure,
orientation, willingness, or consent; absent or variable genital response does
not by itself establish lack of interest or a disorder. Treat the user's felt
experience and communicated choice as distinct from physiology.

Use this model to reduce shame and mistaken self-interpretation, not to dismiss
pain, sudden change, persistent difficulty, or concern about a medical factor.
Those concerns still require proportionate medical triage.

## Life-course and access map

When sexuality changes around pregnancy, postpartum recovery, menopause,
ageing, disability, chronic illness, intensive treatment, or surgery, map:

- what changed and when, without assuming one cause;
- pain, fatigue, sleep, mobility, sensation, body image, mood, privacy,
  caregiving load, relationship context, and access barriers;
- what forms of intimacy, pleasure, or connection the user values now;
- medical symptoms or treatment effects that require qualified assessment;
- accessible, freely chosen options and a no-change option.

Do not frame sexuality as mandatory at any life stage, erase asexual or
aromantic identity, assume disability prevents sexual agency, or attribute a
change to hormones, ageing, or psychology without assessment.

## Evidence and limits

Links checked 2026-08-25.

- Basson's circular model highlights responsive desire and overlapping phases,
  while later population work supports heterogeneity rather than one universal
  sequence:
  [Basson, 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11898699/) and
  [Nowosielski et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26601676/).
- The dual control model describes sexual response as the balance of excitation
  and inhibition; a 2023 scoping review found broad research use alongside
  important variation in measures and applications:
  [Janssen & Bancroft, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37267113/).
- A meta-analysis of laboratory studies found imperfect agreement between
  genital and self-reported arousal. It supports separating these signals, not
  predicting one person's response from a group average:
  [Chivers et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20049519/).
- The 2024 International Consultation on Sexual Medicine recommends
  biopsychosocial, individualized assessment, including chronic illness and
  relationship context, while acknowledging that some recommendations rely on
  expert consensus:
  [Brotto et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39786497/).

These sources concern human research and professional care. They support
careful psychoeducation and formulation but do not validate this AI skill as a
sex therapist or establish effectiveness for an individual user.
